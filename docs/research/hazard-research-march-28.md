## Section 1 — Comprehensive UK Dog Walking Hazard Audit

### BIOLOGICAL AND WILDLIFE HAZARDS

---

**TICKS (Ixodes ricinus — sheep tick; Ixodes hexagonus — hedgehog tick)**

Seasonal window: March-October active; year-round at low level; peak May-June.
Terrain: Woodland edges, bracken, long grass, heathland, moorland. Any habitat with deer or sheep.
Severity: HIGH (Lyme disease risk to dog and owner; Babesia canis risk in SE England).
In app: NO.

The sheep tick (Ixodes ricinus) is the UK's primary tick species and the main vector for Lyme disease (Borrelia burgdorferi). Approximately 15% of UK dogs are infested at any one time. Lyme disease causes approximately 1,500 laboratory-confirmed human cases per year in England and Wales, with an additional 1,000-4,000 clinical cases. Records of tick activity peak in May and June. High-risk areas include the Scottish Highlands (44.1 Lyme cases per 100,000 population), New Forest, Exmoor, Dartmoor, the South Downs, Surrey/Hampshire/Dorset/Somerset, the Lake District, and the Cotswolds.

The hedgehog tick (Ixodes hexagonus) is less commonly associated with Lyme disease but is found in urban and suburban gardens where hedgehogs are present. Ticks require a minimum of 36 hours attached before transmitting Lyme disease.

Tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV) is an emerging concern in the UK; the UKHSA Tick Surveillance Scheme identified risk areas in southern England and Yorkshire.

Action if tick found: Use a tick removal tool (O'Tom Tick Twister, Tick Tornado, Tick Card). Grasp close to skin; do not squeeze the body; do not twist; pull upward with steady pressure. Do NOT use Vaseline, burning, nail varnish, or squeezing. Monitor for Lyme symptoms (bulls-eye rash at bite site, lethargy, lameness, swollen joints) for 30 days. Consult vet.

Prevention: Monthly/quarterly spot-on or tablet treatments. Bravecto (fluralaner) lasts 3 months. Seresto collar (imidacloprid + flumethrin) lasts 8 months. Frontline (fipronil) monthly. Lyme disease vaccination for dogs is available via vet in high-risk areas.

**ADDERS (Vipera berus)**

Seasonal window: April-June (emergence from hibernation; most bites occur May-June). Adults may be seen March-September.
Terrain: Heathland, moorland, chalk downland, sunny banks, woodland edges.
Severity: HIGH (potentially fatal without treatment, especially small dogs).
In app: YES (Apr-Jun).

Already covered. Confirm trigger logic is Apr-Jun only and not year-round. Adders are most active and defensive in spring when first emerging.

---

**HARVEST MITES (Neotrombicula autumnalis)**

Seasonal window: August-October.
Terrain: Chalk and limestone grassland, heathland, woodland margins, dry meadows.
Severity: MEDIUM (intense irritation; secondary infection risk).
In app: YES (Aug-Oct).

Already covered. Larvae cluster in bright orange groups on paws, ears, and belly. Cause intense pruritus. Dogs should be checked after walks through long grass in affected areas, particularly in SE England.

---

**GRASS SEEDS AND AWNS (foxtail barley, wild barley, brome grasses)**

Seasonal window: June-August.
Terrain: Long grass, field margins, path edges, meadows.
Severity: MEDIUM-HIGH (penetration injuries: ears, eyes, paws, nose, skin).
In app: YES (Jun-Aug).

Already covered. Awns travel forward only; once embedded they migrate inward through tissue. Ears are highest risk. Post-walk checks essential. Long-haired breeds particularly vulnerable.

---

**BLUE-GREEN ALGAE (cyanobacteria)**

Seasonal window: May-September (temp ≥20°C).
Terrain: Still or slow-moving water: lakes, reservoirs, ponds, canals.
Severity: CRITICAL (lethal; dogs can die within hours).
In app: YES (May-Sep, temp-gated).

Already covered. Even small amounts of contaminated water can be fatal. Cyanotoxins survive in dead algae. Do not let dogs swim in or drink from affected water.

---

**OAK PROCESSIONARY MOTH CATERPILLARS (Thaumetopoea processionea)**

Seasonal window: May-July (caterpillar stage; adult moths July-August).
Terrain: Oak woodland and parkland, primarily SE England and Greater London. Expanding range.
Severity: HIGH (urticating hairs cause severe allergic reactions; can cause anaphylaxis).
In app: NO.

OPM caterpillars were first recorded in SW London in 2005. They are now established across Greater London and surrounding counties (Kent, Surrey, Berkshire, Hertfordshire, Buckinghamshire). The silken nests (white, pear-shaped) are visible on oak tree trunks and branches. Contact with the hairs causes: drooling, swelling of the tongue and gums, vomiting, respiratory distress, eye inflammation. Even contact with shed hairs on the ground is hazardous. Dead nests retain hazardous hairs for months.

Action: Do not let dogs sniff or investigate white silken nests on oak trees. Keep dogs on lead in oak woodland from May-July in SE England. If contact suspected: flush mouth and eyes with water, seek emergency vet attention. Report sightings to Forestry Commission via TreeAlert.

---

**LUNGWORM (Angiostrongylus vasorum)**

Seasonal window: Year-round; peak summer-autumn when slugs are most abundant.
Terrain: Woodland, gardens, any damp ground with slug/snail activity.
Severity: HIGH (potentially fatal if untreated; difficult to diagnose early).
In app: NO.

Lungworm is transmitted via accidental ingestion of infected slugs, snails, or frog. Dogs may eat slugs while foraging, drink water containing slug trails, or play with/lick slugs. The parasite is endemic in SE England, Greater London, West Midlands, and South Wales. Greater London has very high prevalence in the local fox population (a wildlife reservoir). Symptoms: coughing, breathlessness, poor clotting, weight loss, seizures, lethargy.

Prevention: Monthly treatments with milbemycin oxime (Milbemax) or moxidectin (Advocate) in endemic areas. Standard worming (fenbendazole) does NOT prevent lungworm. Do not leave water bowls outdoors overnight. Discourage dogs from eating slugs.

**BABESIA CANIS (via Dermacentor reticulatus tick)**

Seasonal window: March-October (aligned with tick season).
Terrain: Woodland and scrub; currently centred on Harlow/Essex area of SE England.
Severity: HIGH (destroys red blood cells; can be fatal).
In app: NO.

Babesia canis causes canine babesiosis: destruction of red blood cells leading to anaemia, jaundice, and organ failure. The first UK endemic focus was established in Harlow, Essex in winter 2015/16, via Dermacentor reticulatus ticks (a different tick species from the common sheep tick). This tick species is found at higher densities in parts of SE England. Cases remain geographically limited but the risk is real in Essex and potentially expanding areas.

Symptoms: lethargy, pale gums, lack of appetite, fever, jaundice, enlarged abdomen, weight loss.

This is best addressed as part of a tick deep dive rather than a standalone hazard alert, but warrants a specific mention in tick content for dogs in SE England and Essex particularly.

**FUNGI AND MUSHROOMS**

Seasonal window: August-November (peak September-October).
Terrain: Broadleaved woodland (death cap, funeral bell), coniferous woodland, churchyards, anywhere with decaying organic matter.
Severity: CRITICAL (death cap can be lethal from half a cap; delayed organ failure).
In app: NO.

UK toxic fungi hazardous to dogs:

- Death cap (Amanita phalloides): grows in broadleaved woodland August-November. Most deadly fungus in the world. Symptoms delayed 6-24 hours; initial vomiting and diarrhoea followed by apparent recovery, then liver and kidney failure. Ingestion of half a cap can be lethal.
- Funeral bell (Galerina marginata): coniferous woodland, August-November. Contains same amatoxins as death cap.
- Fly agaric (Amanita muscaria): iconic red/white-spotted. Causes neurological signs (stumbling, seizures, confusion) rather than organ failure. Rarely fatal.
- False death cap (Amanita citrina): often mistaken for death cap; less toxic but still hazardous.

Action: Seek emergency vet treatment immediately if mushroom ingestion suspected. Do not wait for symptoms. Early decontamination is critical. There is no antidote to amatoxin; treatment is supportive.

---

**TOXIC PLANTS**

Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea): Woodland edges, heathland, moorland. Flowers April-September. All parts toxic (cardiac glycosides causing heart arrhythmia). Severity HIGH.

Ragwort (Jacobaea vulgaris): Farmland, rough grassland. Flowers June-September. Cumulative liver damage. Dogs rarely eat enough during a walk to be immediately dangerous but repeat exposure is a risk. Severity MEDIUM.

Acorns (Quercus spp.): Oak woodland September-November. Tannic acid causes vomiting, diarrhoea, kidney damage from large quantities. Many dogs snack on acorns without severe effect; risk scales with quantity consumed. Severity MEDIUM.

Conkers (Aesculus hippocastanum): Horse chestnut woodland and parks, September-November. Aesculin causes vomiting, diarrhoea. Rarely fatal; hazard often overstated. Severity LOW-MEDIUM.

Wild garlic/Allium family: Spring woodland. All Allium plants (wild garlic, wild onion) cause haemolytic anaemia. Often grows in large patches in UK woodland.

All high-risk plants should be discussed in walk page content where relevant; a dedicated guide covering the top 10 wild plants toxic to dogs is warranted.

**LEPTOSPIROSIS (Weil's disease)**

Seasonal window: Year-round; higher risk in wetter months (autumn-spring). Also peaks after flooding.
Terrain: Rivers, ponds, canals, standing water, farmland, urban water-adjacent areas.
Severity: HIGH (organ failure; zoonotic - humans can also contract from contaminated water).
In app: NO.

Spread via urine from infected rats and other mammals contaminating water and soil. Bacteria survives for months in water and soil. High-risk activities: swimming in rivers/canals, drinking from stagnant water, walking through flooded areas. Annual L4 vaccination (Nobivac L4 or Versican) provides good protection. Urban dogs in areas with high rat populations are at risk year-round.

Symptoms: vomiting, diarrhoea, lethargy, jaundice, muscle pain, kidney/liver failure.

Note: Leptospirosis is zoonotic. The same water that risks a dog can risk the owner.

### ENVIRONMENTAL HAZARDS

---

**ANTIFREEZE (ethylene glycol)**

Seasonal window: November-March (peak December-February around car maintenance season).
Terrain: Urban and suburban: driveways, car parks, garages, roadsides.
Severity: CRITICAL (rapid organ failure; commonly fatal; half a teaspoon per pound bodyweight can kill).
In app: NO.

Ethylene glycol tastes sweet, which actively attracts dogs. Found in: car antifreeze, screen wash, brake fluid, radiator coolant, garden water feature antifreeze. Symptoms appear within 30-60 minutes: stumbling/ataxia, lethargy, vomiting. Brief apparent recovery period followed by acute kidney failure within 24-72 hours. Treatment must begin within 12 hours of ingestion; survival rate drops sharply after this window.

Highest risk: spills in car parks, driveways after car maintenance, puddles under parked cars. Propylene glycol-based antifreeze is safer but still not entirely non-toxic.

Action: Emergency vet immediately. This is one of the most time-critical poisoning scenarios.

---

**HOT PAVEMENT PAW BURNS**

Seasonal window: May-September (when air temperature ≥25°C and sun is shining).
Terrain: Tarmac, asphalt, paving stones, artificial grass. Worst on south-facing surfaces.
Severity: HIGH (second-degree burns possible within 60 seconds on surfaces at 52°C+).
In app: PARTIAL (heat-paw alert exists but not tied specifically to pavement temperature threshold).

At 25°C air temperature, asphalt can reach 52°C. At 35°C air temperature, asphalt can reach 65°C. Burns can occur in under 60 seconds at 49°C. The 7-second hand test: place the back of your hand on the pavement. If it is too painful to hold for 7 seconds, it is too painful for your dog's paws.

Artificial grass is consistently hotter than asphalt or tarmac. Natural grass stays cool through transpiration.

Action: Walk on grass where possible; walk early morning or evening; use dog booties; carry water to pour on hot surfaces ahead of the dog.

Note: The existing heat-paw alert should be reviewed to confirm the temperature trigger is correctly calibrated at ≥25°C air temperature (not requiring higher thresholds).

**RAT POISON AND ANTICOAGULANT RODENTICIDES**

Seasonal window: Year-round (urban); note: from July 2024, stricter UK regulations restrict outdoor use of second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides.
Terrain: Urban parks, allotments, farmland, woodland edges with bait stations.
Severity: HIGH (anticoagulant effect causes internal bleeding over days; difficult to diagnose until advanced).
In app: NO.

Second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides (SGARs) including brodifacoum, bromadiolone, and difenacoum are commonly used in tamper-resistant bait stations in parks, allotments, and alongside paths. Dogs can directly ingest bait from poorly secured stations or eat poisoned rodents (secondary poisoning). Symptoms: bleeding from gums or nose, bruising, lethargy, appearing pale, bloody stools. Symptoms can be delayed 3-7 days, making diagnosis difficult.

From July 2024: new UK CRRU regulations restrict outdoor use of brodifacoum and flocoumafen. However, bromadiolone and difenacoum remain permitted for outdoor use, meaning the hazard persists.

Action: Emergency vet if ingestion suspected. Vitamin K treatment is effective if started early.

---

**CANNABIS AND DRUG LITTER**

Seasonal window: Year-round (increasing trend; not seasonal).
Terrain: Urban parks, paths, car parks, urban green spaces.
Severity: MEDIUM (distressing for dog; rarely fatal from cannabis itself; higher risk from other drug residues).
In app: NO.

Over 450 dogs treated for cannabis poisoning in the UK over the past four years; cases are rising. Dogs may ingest discarded joints, cannabis resin, or edibles (particularly brownies and gummies containing THC). Symptoms: ataxia (drunk walking), lethargy, dilated pupils, vomiting, disorientation, sometimes seizures. Onset: 30-90 minutes when ingested; 6-12 minutes if inhaled. Rarely fatal from cannabis alone; however, edibles may also contain xylitol (highly toxic to dogs).

Action: Vet visit or Vets Now if symptoms appear. Treatment supportive. Note: owners should not delay vet visit due to embarrassment about the cause.

**JELLYFISH (coastal beaches)**

Seasonal window: Late July-January; peak sightings August-October; Portuguese man o' war peak October-January (driven by Atlantic winds).
Terrain: Sandy beaches and tidelines, particularly Atlantic-facing coasts (Devon, Cornwall, Wales, West Scotland).
Severity: MEDIUM-HIGH (Portuguese man o' war can cause severe reaction; common jellyfish mild-moderate).
In app: NO.

Portuguese man o' war sightings increased fourfold in the 2023-2024 period versus 2022-2023. Even dead, beached jellyfish retain their stinging cells. Dogs investigate jellyfish by sniffing and mouthing them; facial stings are the primary risk. Lion's mane jellyfish (large, brown-orange) is the most venomous common UK species. Portuguese man o' war is not technically a jellyfish but is similarly dangerous.

Symptoms: sudden yelping, pawing at face, drooling, swelling.

Action: Flush with sea water (not fresh water); remove tentacles with a stick (do not use bare hands); seek vet advice. Keep dogs on lead near fresh jellyfish on beaches.

This is a coastal walk hazard best addressed via a walk-page hazard pill ("coastal hazards: jellyfish July-January") rather than a weather-triggered app alert.

### LIVESTOCK AND WILDLIFE ENCOUNTERS

---

**CATTLE (particularly with calves)**

Seasonal window: Year-round; highest risk March-June (calving season, spring protectiveness).
Terrain: Farmland fields on public footpaths, moorland.
Severity: CRITICAL (most dangerous animal encounter for UK walkers; dogs are a primary trigger for charges).
In app: NO.

Statistical data: 94% of cattle-related walking injuries involved a dog present. Five people have been killed in cattle incidents recorded over 7 years of data collection; 8% suffered serious injuries (broken bones, concussion, internal injuries). Two thirds of all attacks involved dogs. Cattle perceive dogs as predators; cows with calves are particularly defensive.

Advice: Keep dog on lead when crossing cattle fields. If cattle begin to move toward you, let the dog off the lead so you can both escape separately; do not release the dog until the cattle are actively approaching. Do not run. Move calmly to the field edge. Avoid fields with visible calves where possible.

This is one of the highest-severity hazards for walking dog owners in the UK. A dedicated guide and a walk-page "livestock present" tag are warranted. Year-round hazard with seasonal elevation.

---

**DEER RUT**

Seasonal window: September-November (red deer peak mid-October; fallow deer October-early November; roe deer July-August for buck rut).
Terrain: Parkland (Richmond Park, Bushy Park, Tatton Park), woodland, moorland, deer parks.
Severity: HIGH (rutting stags charge; antler injuries can be fatal).
In app: NO.

Rutting stags are driven by testosterone and territorial instinct. A red deer stag can weigh over 320kg (25 stone) and reach 48kph. Dogs not on leads that approach deer during the rut can provoke charges. The Royal Parks (Richmond, Bushy) issue specific warnings every October. Royal Parks advice: keep dogs on lead, maintain 50m minimum distance from deer, do not get between a stag and his harem.

Action: If stag approaches, back away slowly. If charged, release the dog (dogs can outrun deer). Do not shout or wave arms.

---

**SHEEP LAMBING SEASON**

Seasonal window: February-April (lowland flocks from February; upland flocks from March-April).
Terrain: Farmland, upland fields with public access.
Severity: MEDIUM (dogs chasing sheep during lambing is illegal; pregnant ewes can abort from stress).
In app: NO.

Under the Dogs (Protection of Livestock) Act 1953, a farmer is legally entitled to shoot a dog that is worrying livestock. Dogs perceived as threatening to sheep, even without physical contact, can cause ewes to abort. Keep dogs on lead near sheep year-round; this is especially critical during lambing.

---

**NESTING GROUND BIRDS**

Seasonal window: March-June.
Terrain: Heathland, moorland, farmland, coastal grassland.
Severity: LOW (hazard is to wildlife, not the dog; but disturbing Schedule 1 species nests is a criminal offence).
In app: NO.

Lapwing, skylark, and curlew all nest on the ground. Dog disturbance during nesting causes nest abandonment and chick loss. Many nature reserves operate seasonal on-lead requirements. Not a safety hazard to dogs but a legal and ethical consideration. Mention in walk pages and editorial guides; not warranted as an in-app alert.

---

**SHOOTING SEASONS**

Seasonal window: Red grouse Aug 12-Dec 10; partridge Sep 1-Feb 1; pheasant Oct 1-Feb 1; deer stalking year-round varies by species.
Terrain: Moorland (grouse), farmland and woodland (pheasant, partridge), open countryside (deer).
Severity: MEDIUM (risk of proximity to live shooting; startled dogs running off).
In app: NO.

Dog walkers on rights of way during shooting seasons should be aware of shooting activity. Shoots are required not to fire across footpaths in use, but incidents can occur. Bright, visible clothing and keeping dogs on leads during game shooting season on moorland and farmland is advisable. The primary risk is a dog running off when startled by gunshots, or entering a shooting line.

### WEATHER-RELATED (BEYOND CURRENT COVERAGE)

---

**UV SUNBURN**

Seasonal window: May-September (peak June-August, particularly 11am-3pm).
Terrain: All, particularly open ground, beaches, south-facing slopes.
Severity: MEDIUM (skin damage; squamous cell carcinoma risk from repeated sunburn).
In app: NO.

At-risk dogs: pink or light-pigmented nose, white or light-coloured coat, sparse coat, no undercoat, hairless breeds. Most vulnerable areas: nose, ear tips, eyelids, belly/groin. Human sunscreen must NOT be used on dogs (zinc oxide is toxic). Pet-safe sun cream at SPF30+ is available from vets and online.

Existing heat alerts partially cover this period, but UV sunburn is a distinct risk (can occur on a cool but sunny day at UV Index 4+). Guide article yes; app alert low priority.

---

**POLLEN AND SEASONAL ALLERGIES**

Seasonal window: March-September. Tree pollen Mar-May; grass pollen Jun-Aug; weed pollen Aug-Sep.
Terrain: Grassland, parkland, gardens.
Severity: LOW (chronic discomfort; secondary skin infections; rarely serious).
In app: NO.

Unlike humans, dogs primarily experience pollen allergy as a skin condition (atopic dermatitis), not respiratory. Symptoms: itching/licking/rubbing paws, ears, muzzle, belly; hair loss from rubbing; flaky or greasy-feeling skin. Wiping paws and coat after walks with a damp cloth reduces pollen load. Avoid rolling in grass.

Treatment options: antihistamines, medicated shampoos, apoquel, cytopoint injections. Not warranted as an in-app alert; guide article appropriate.

---

**FAST WATER AND FLOOD RISK**

Seasonal window: Autumn-Spring (high-rainfall periods; November-February peak).
Terrain: Rivers, streams, fords, coastal paths, flood plains.
Severity: HIGH (dogs swept away in fast water; hypothermia in cold water).
In app: NO.

After heavy rainfall, rivers that appear calm can have strong subsurface currents. Waterfalls and weirs create powerful undertows. Dogs that enter rivers after rainfall can be swept away even in familiar spots. Hypothermia risk is high in cold, fast-moving water (UK water temperature December-March is 5-8°C).

Action: Keep dogs on lead near rivers after heavy rain; do not let dogs enter flooded fields; be aware of flood warnings for river valleys.

---

**AGRICULTURAL CHEMICALS**

Seasonal window: March-October (main spray season; autumn herbicide application).
Terrain: Farmland, field margins, path edges adjacent to agricultural land.
Severity: MEDIUM (acute exposure low-risk from a single walk; chronic exposure higher risk from regular contact).
In app: NO.

UK councils and land managers are not required to provide advance warning before pesticide application. Herbicide (2,4-D) exposure has been associated with increased lymphoma risk in dogs from chronic exposure. Residues can persist on grass for weeks after application. Walk path edges and hard surfaces rather than through grass on agricultural land where possible; wipe paws after farm walks.

Not warranted as an in-app hazard alert (too diffuse and untriggerable without specific location data); mention in farmland walk page content.

### URBAN AND SUBURBAN HAZARDS

---

**DISCARDED FOOD AND XYLITOL**

Seasonal window: Year-round (BBQ season June-August increases hazard density in parks).
Terrain: Urban parks, paths, car parks, picnic areas, beaches.
Severity: HIGH for xylitol (can cause acute liver failure; found in chewing gum, sugar-free sweets, peanut butter); MEDIUM for cooked bones (splintering), grapes/raisins (renal failure).
In app: NO.

Xylitol is found in: sugar-free chewing gum (Orbit, Extra), sugar-free mints, some peanut butter brands, protein bars. As little as 1-3 pieces of gum can cause hypoglycaemia in a medium dog. Grapes and raisins cause acute renal failure; the toxic dose is unknown and any amount should be treated seriously.

BBQ season (June-August) increases discarded cooked chicken bones, corn on the cob (intestinal blockage), and other food hazards in parks. Corn on the cob is the most common cause of intestinal obstruction requiring surgery in UK dogs.

## Section 3 — Tick Deep Dive (Content Brief for Priority Guide Article)

### UK Tick Species

**Ixodes ricinus (sheep tick / deer tick / castor bean tick):** The dominant UK species and main Lyme disease vector. Found throughout the UK wherever deer, sheep, or small mammals are present. Up to 3mm when unfed; engorges to 11mm when full. All life stages (larva, nymph, adult) bite. Nymphs are most commonly responsible for Lyme disease transmission as they are tiny (1-2mm) and easily missed.

**Ixodes hexagonus (hedgehog tick):** Primarily associated with hedgehogs and other wildlife. Found in urban and suburban gardens. Less commonly associated with Lyme disease transmission but can bite dogs.

**Dermacentor reticulatus (ornate dog tick):** The species responsible for Babesia canis transmission in the Essex cluster. Larger than Ixodes species. Currently restricted in UK distribution but monitoring ongoing.

### Seasonal Activity Calendar

| Month | Activity Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| January | Low | Quiescent in most of UK |
| February | Low-medium | Active on mild days (>5°C) |
| March | Medium | Adults active; nymphs emerging |
| April | HIGH | Peak spring activity begins |
| May | PEAK | Highest activity; nymph peak |
| June | PEAK | Highest activity; nymph peak |
| July | Medium-high | Activity reduces in hot dry weather |
| August | Medium | Second activity rise in wetter conditions |
| September | Medium-high | Autumn activity rise |
| October | Medium | Adults seeking hosts before winter |
| November | Low-medium | Reducing but active on mild days |
| December | Low | Mostly quiescent |

Climate change is extending the tick season, with active ticks now recorded in mild January and February conditions. Year-round vigilance is increasingly warranted.

### Habitat Types

Ticks require high humidity to survive. Prime habitats:
- **Bracken** - the single highest-risk vegetation type. Bracken creates the perfect microclimate: humid, shaded, high wildlife density.
- **Woodland edges** - the interface between woodland and open ground has the highest tick density. Walking through the canopy interior is lower risk than the edge.
- **Long grass and meadows** - ticks quest (climb to tips of grass and outstretched legs) waiting for passing hosts.
- **Heathland** - particularly heather and gorse habitats with deer and ponies.
- **Moorland** - particularly where deer, red grouse, and mountain hares are present.
- **Urban parks and gardens** - where hedgehog ticks are present; deer have been recorded in suburban areas.

### How Ticks Attach and Feed

Ticks do not jump or fall from trees. They quest: they climb vegetation (typically 10-60cm height), extend their legs, and wait to grasp a passing host. Once on a host, they move toward warm, dark, thin-skinned areas to feed. Feeding takes 3-7 days. Crucially: transmission of Lyme disease requires the tick to be attached for a minimum of 36 hours. Early removal substantially reduces transmission risk.

### Body Map: Where Ticks Hide on Dogs

Post-walk tick check - prioritise:
1. Around and inside the ears
2. Under the collar
3. Armpits (axillae)
4. Between the toes and under paw pads
5. Groin and inner thighs
6. Around the tail base and under the tail
7. Eyelids and around the muzzle
8. Any skin folds

Run fingers through the coat slowly; ticks feel like a small bump. Engorged ticks are easier to find than newly attached ones.

### Correct Tick Removal

**Use a tick removal tool:** O'Tom Tick Twister and Tick Tornado are widely available (£4-8). These hook under the tick and allow a smooth lifting rotation without squeezing the body.

**With fine-pointed tweezers:** Grasp as close to the skin as possible. Pull upward with slow, steady pressure. Do not jerk or twist.

**Critical: Do NOT:**
- Squeeze the body of the tick (forces infectious saliva into the bite)
- Twist or rotate the tick
- Apply Vaseline, butter, or petroleum jelly
- Use a lit match or cigarette end to burn the tick
- Use nail varnish or alcohol on an attached tick

**After removal:** Place the tick in a sealed container or plastic bag. Note the date. Monitor the dog for 30 days. If a bulls-eye (expanding ring) rash appears at the bite site, or if the dog shows lameness, lethargy, or swollen joints, consult a vet immediately.

### Tick-Borne Diseases in UK Dogs

**Lyme disease (Borrelia burgdorferi s.l.)**
Most common tick-borne disease in the UK. Estimated 1,500 confirmed + 1,000-4,000 clinical human cases per year. In dogs: affects joints, nervous system, kidneys. Symptoms: intermittent lameness, swollen joints, lethargy, fever, anorexia, kidney disease in chronic cases. Diagnosis: blood test. Treatment: antibiotics (doxycycline). Prognosis: good with early treatment; renal Lyme is more serious.

**Babesiosis (Babesia canis via Dermacentor reticulatus)**
Emerging in UK; currently centred on Harlow/Essex area. Causes destruction of red blood cells. Symptoms: lethargy, pale/yellow gums, weakness, fever. Can be rapidly fatal. Treatment: antiprotozoal drugs. Blood transfusion may be needed. Urgent vet care required.

**Anaplasmosis (Anaplasma phagocytophilum)**
Affects white blood cells. Symptoms: fever, lethargy, joint pain, vomiting, anorexia. UK cases confirmed but relatively rare. Treatment: doxycycline.

### Lyme Disease in Humans

The same I. ricinus ticks that bite dogs also bite the humans walking with them. A dog that brings ticks back from a walk can transfer them to the owner. UK Lyme disease in humans causes: characteristic bulls-eye (erythema migrans) rash in 70-80% of cases; flu-like illness; if untreated, joint, neurological, and cardiac complications. Annual human Lyme cases in Scotland (1,000+ per year) and England/Wales (1,500+ confirmed). Owners who walk in tick-prone areas should also check themselves post-walk.

## Section 4 — Alabama Rot (CRGV) Specific Research

### Current Understanding

Alabama rot (Cutaneous and Renal Glomerular Vasculopathy, CRGV) is a disease causing microscopic clots in small blood vessels, leading to skin ulceration and kidney failure. It first appeared in Greyhounds in Alabama, US in the 1980s. It was first confirmed in UK dogs in 2012; the UK strain is now considered distinct and potentially different in cause from the original US disease.

**Total confirmed UK cases:** 334 as of early 2025 (10 in 2024; 6 in early 2025 by February).
**Survival rate:** Approximately 30% of dogs referred to the RVC Queen Mother Hospital with CRGV survive.

### Cause: Unknown

The cause of UK CRGV remains unconfirmed. Current theories:
- Toxins produced by E. coli bacteria (E. coli NOT found in UK histological examinations; described as "suspected CRGV" rather than confirmed Alabama rot)
- Environmental toxin triggered by specific ground conditions (wet, cold woodland soil)
- Bacterial infection (Streptococcus, Rickettsia investigated; none confirmed as primary cause)
- University of Bristol has had "promising early results" with a bacterial candidate as of 2023-2024; confirmation work ongoing

Since cause is unknown, definitive prevention advice cannot be given.

### Seasonal Pattern

Over 90% of UK cases occur between November and May. Peak December-March coincides with the wettest and coldest months. Summer months are near-zero risk. This strong seasonal pattern suggests an environmental trigger activated by cold, wet conditions - most plausibly a soil-borne organism or toxin that is active in cold, waterlogged ground.

### Geographic Pattern

Cases have been confirmed across most of England, with notable clusters in:
- New Forest area (Hampshire)
- South West England (Devon, Somerset, Dorset, Wiltshire)
- South East (Surrey, Berkshire, Kent)
- Midlands (Staffordshire, Warwickshire)
- Yorkshire and North West
- London and surrounding counties

No region of England is entirely free from cases. Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland have far fewer confirmed cases but cases have been reported.

### Terrain Association

A statistically significant association with woodland walking, particularly on muddy ground in winter, is the most consistent finding in published research. Research published in BMJ (2018) found cases were associated with: woodland terrain, increasing maximum temperatures in winter/spring/autumn, increasing rainfall in winter/spring, decreasing livestock density.

The New Forest area has consistently had the highest case concentration relative to dog population.

### Symptoms to Watch For

**Stage 1 - Skin lesions (appear first, 1-9 days before kidney failure):**
- Ulcerated or sore-looking lesions on lower legs, paws, and occasionally face, mouth, tongue
- Lesions appear as circular, punched-out ulcers
- Often described as looking like a burn or chemical injury
- Not healing normally

**Stage 2 - Kidney failure (typically 2-9 days after skin lesions appear):**
- Vomiting
- Lethargy
- Loss of appetite
- Excessive thirst and urination, then reduced urination
- Jaundice in severe cases

Key: The skin lesions appear BEFORE kidney failure. Early recognition of skin lesions and immediate vet referral is critical.

### Veterinary Guidance (Current, 2024-2025)

- No confirmed preventative treatment.
- Washing legs and paws after muddy woodland walks is recommended as precautionary measure even without evidence of direct benefit; it allows earlier detection of skin lesions.
- If any skin lesion is found on legs/paws/muzzle, seek veterinary advice immediately.
- Dogs with active skin lesions from CRGV are likely to be referred to specialist internal medicine service for monitoring and possible treatment.
- Plasma exchange therapy (TPE) has shown promise as a novel treatment in survival cases; it remains experimental.

### In-App Hazard vs Guide Article

**Recommendation: Both, but with care about framing.**

A seasonal advisory is warranted for woodland walks November-May with a very specific, calm framing. Alabama rot is rare (334 cases over 12 years across millions of dog walks). Alarmist framing would be irresponsible and likely inaccurate. The correct message is simple, practical, and reassuring.

In-app: A seasonal "rinse after muddy woodland walks" advisory, November-May, for walks tagged as woodland. Do not name Alabama rot specifically in the short alert copy (creates disproportionate alarm). Use it in longer content.

Guide article: Yes - a well-written, accurate, reassuring guide that covers what is known, what the symptoms are, and what to do. This is the most-searched dog health topic during the Nov-May window in the UK and has high search volume.
