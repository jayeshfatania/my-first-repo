# Sniffout — Hazard Content Tone of Voice Guide
**Version:** 1.0
**Date:** 28 March 2026
**Applies to:** In-app hazard alerts, walk page hazard pills, companion website hazard guide articles

---

## The Core Principle

Sniffout hazard content exists to help dog owners make better decisions — not to frighten them. Every piece of hazard content we write should leave the reader feeling informed and capable, not anxious and overwhelmed.

The test for every piece of hazard copy: **would a knowledgeable friend say this to you?**

A knowledgeable friend who happens to be a vet would not say "Ticks can transmit Lyme disease, Babesia canis, Tick-borne encephalitis and Anaplasmosis to your dog." They would say "Worth checking Biscuit after that walk — good spot for ticks around here."

---

## Who We Are Talking To

A dog owner who loves their dog, takes their walks seriously, and wants to be informed without being lectured. They are not ignorant — they know hazards exist. They do not need fear to motivate action. What they need is practical, specific, trustworthy guidance that fits naturally into their walk.

They chose Sniffout partly because it feels honest and personal, not corporate or alarmist. Every hazard message must earn its place in that relationship.

---

## The Three Rules

### Rule 1 - Practical over alarming

Every hazard message must end with something the owner can actually do. If we cannot tell the owner what to do about it, we should not mention it.

Good: "After walks in bracken and long grass, check [dog name] thoroughly - especially around the ears, neck, and between toes."

Bad: "Ticks are a serious public health risk and can transmit multiple life-threatening diseases to dogs and humans."

The first is useful. The second is true but leaves the owner with nowhere to go.

### Rule 2 - Honest about risk levels

We do not amplify hazards beyond their actual risk. Alabama rot has occurred in 334 confirmed cases over 12 years across millions of UK dog walks. That is serious enough to mention - it is not serious enough to lead with "DANGER: FATAL DISEASE." The honest framing builds more trust than the alarming one, and it is more accurate.

When the evidence is uncertain, say so. "Vets are not completely sure what causes Alabama rot, but muddy woodland walks in winter seem to be a factor. Rinsing [dog name]'s paws and legs after these walks is the practical advice for now."

That honesty is differentiating. Most pet content amplifies uncertainty into fear. We do the opposite.

### Rule 3 - Dog-first, always

The dog is the subject of every hazard message, not the hazard. We write about [dog name], not about pathogens, toxins, or statistics. The hazard is context. The dog is the story.

Good: "[Dog name] can pick up lungworm from eating slugs or snails - worth discouraging that habit, especially on damp evening walks."

Bad: "Angiostrongylus vasorum is a parasitic roundworm transmitted via ingestion of infected molluscs."

## In-App Alert Copy Rules

In-app alerts appear as a pill or card on the Today tab during the relevant season. They must be:

- 1-3 sentences maximum
- Actionable in one step
- Uses [dog name] - always personalised, never "your dog"
- No em dashes - hyphens only
- No exclamation marks
- No medical terminology
- No statistics

### In-app alert template

[Season/terrain context]. Check [dog name] [specific action]. [What to look for or do if found.]

Examples:

Tick season is here. After walks in woodland or long grass, check [dog name] carefully around the ears, neck, armpits, and between toes. Remove any ticks with a tick removal tool, twisting gently rather than pulling.

Autumn mushroom season. Keep [dog name] away from mushrooms in woodland - some UK species are highly toxic and symptoms can be delayed by several hours. If you think they have eaten one, call your vet straight away.

Antifreeze risk over winter. Keep [dog name] away from puddles near parked cars and driveways - antifreeze tastes sweet to dogs and is extremely dangerous. If you are worried they have licked some, get to a vet immediately.

## Walk Page Hazard Pill Rules

Hazard pills appear on individual walk pages on the companion website. They flag specific, known hazards for that route. They are static, walk-specific data - not weather-triggered.

Pills should be:
- One line maximum
- Specific to the walk, not generic
- Seasonally qualified where relevant

Examples:

Deer rut - Oct-Nov - Keep extra distance from stags
Cattle fields on route - Calving Mar-Jun - Keep on lead
Oak woodland - OPM caterpillars May-Jul - Do not let dogs investigate tree trunks
Coastal stretch - Jellyfish Jul-Jan - Keep away from beached jellyfish

## Website Guide Article Rules

Guide articles are the full-length companion website content at sniffout.co.uk/guides/. They should feel like a well-researched, warmly written long read - not a medical information sheet.

### Structure

Every guide article follows this structure:

1. Opening paragraph - what this hazard is, told through the lens of a dog on a walk
2. Practical core - what to do, when, and how
3. Honest risk context - how serious is this actually, with evidence
4. UK-specific detail - hotspot regions, seasonal calendar, specific locations
5. What to do if the worst happens - short, clear, one action: call your vet
6. Walk-specific call to action - link to relevant Sniffout walks

### What makes Sniffout articles distinctive

Walk-specific. Our articles link to actual walks in the database. A tick guide links to high-risk walks in the Surrey Hills and New Forest. No competitor can do this.

Honest calibration. We are the publication that says "Alabama rot is serious and rare. Here is what actually matters." That voice is genuinely different from every other source on this topic.

Dog named throughout. Where other guides say "your dog," our articles use [dog name] throughout.

Written by real walkers. Every guide is attributed to a Sniffout persona with a named dog. The reader builds a relationship with Morag from the Peak District, not with "the Sniffout editorial team."

### Tone tests for articles

Read the article back. Ask:

- Could this have been written by the NHS or a vet practice? If yes, it is too clinical. Rewrite it warmer.
- Does it leave the reader more anxious than when they started? If yes, the risk framing is off. Add honest context.
- Is there at least one point where the reader smiles or feels reassured? There should be.
- Does it mention a specific UK place or time of year? It should.
- Is there something in here that only Sniffout could have written? If not, add it.

## What We Never Do

- We never use hazard content to drive anxiety-based engagement
- We never overstate risk to increase time-in-app
- We never write about hazards we have not researched properly
- We never use the word "deadly" in short copy
- We never tell owners what they already know without adding something new

---

## Severity Vocabulary

Use these consistently across all hazard content:

| Severity | In-app language | Article language |
|----------|----------------|-----------------|
| Low | "Worth knowing" | "Generally minor" |
| Medium | "Keep an eye out" | "Can cause discomfort or injury" |
| High | "Take care" | "Serious if untreated" |
| Critical | "Act immediately" | "Seek emergency vet care" |

Never use: deadly, fatal, killer, dangerous in short copy.
In articles: "can be fatal if untreated" is acceptable with context only.

## The Relationship Between App and Website

The app gives a short, timely, personalised heads-up.
The website gives the full picture for those who want it.

Every in-app hazard alert should link to the corresponding guide article on sniffout.co.uk.
