## How to Read This Report

Each alert section lists every factual and instructional claim extracted from the copy as a separate line item. Each claim receives one verdict:

- **CORRECT** - verified against research and authoritative source
- **INCOMPLETE** - factually accurate but missing information that changes the practical meaning or safety of the instruction
- **AMBIGUOUS** - phrasing that could be read in a way that is incorrect or misleading
- **WRONG** - claim is factually incorrect

Each section closes with an overall verdict:

- **PASS** - all claims correct, safe to proceed to Developer
- **FLAG** - fixable issues; must be resolved before proceeding to Developer
- **FAIL** - one or more claims wrong or missing; must not proceed to Developer

## Alert 1 — Ticks

**Copy reviewed:**
"Tick season is underway. After walks in woodland, heathland, or long grass, check [dog name] carefully around the ears, neck, armpits, and between the toes. If you find one, use a tick removal tool and twist gently - do not squeeze or pull straight off."

**Seasonal note reviewed:** "Active March to October."

---

### Claim-by-Claim Verification

**Claim 1.1**
"Tick season is underway."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: The research document confirms the active tick season as March-October. The trigger is set to month 3-10. Appropriate contextual framing for this window.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Ticks): "Seasonal window: March-October active; year-round at low level; peak May-June."

---

**Claim 1.2**
"After walks in woodland, heathland, or long grass..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: All three terrains listed are confirmed high-risk tick habitats. The research notes woodland edges, bracken, long grass, heathland, and moorland as prime habitats. The copy covers three of the five and is appropriate for in-app brevity. Bracken is the single highest-risk vegetation but is encompassed within the general woodland/heathland/long grass framing.
Source: Hazard research, Section 3 (Tick Deep Dive, Habitat Types).

---

**Claim 1.3**
"...check [dog name] carefully around the ears, neck, armpits, and between the toes."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: The research body map lists 8 priority check areas. The copy covers 4 of them: ears, under the collar/neck, armpits, and between the toes. These are the most commonly affected and most commonly missed. Appropriate for in-app brevity. The word "carefully" implies thoroughness without needing to enumerate every location.
Source: Hazard research, Section 3 (Body Map: Where Ticks Hide on Dogs).

---

**Claim 1.4**
"...use a tick removal tool and twist gently..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: The instruction to twist (rather than pull straight) when using a tick removal tool is confirmed by multiple authoritative UK sources. RSPCA: "twist the tick off." Blue Cross: "Twist it in a clockwise direction several times until the tick comes loose." O'Tom Tick Twister manufacturer instructions: "turn it... the tick detaches by itself after 2 or 3 rotations." The "twist gently" instruction in the copy is consistent with all three sources. Note: the research document's action summary in Section 1 states "do not twist" but this instruction applies specifically to tweezer removal, not to tick removal tools. The copy correctly specifies "use a tick removal tool" before giving the twist instruction, which aligns with RSPCA and Blue Cross guidance.
Source: RSPCA Ticks guidance; Blue Cross Ticks on Dogs guidance; O'Tom Tick Twister product instructions; Hazard research, Section 3 (Correct Tick Removal).

---

**Claim 1.5**
"...do not squeeze or pull straight off."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Both negatives are confirmed. Do not squeeze: confirmed by RSPCA ("make sure you don't squeeze the tick's body"), research doc ("do not squeeze the body of the tick"), and O'Tom instructions ("remove it with a twisting motion, without compressing it"). Do not pull straight off: consistent with all source guidance, which specifies twisting/rotating rather than pulling.
Source: RSPCA Ticks guidance; Hazard research, Section 3 (Critical: Do NOT list).

---

**Seasonal note 1.6**
"Active March to October."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Confirmed. Research: "Seasonal window: March-October active." The note is accurate and appropriately concise.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 and Section 3.

---

### Overall Verdict: PASS

All claims in the ticks alert are factually correct and consistent with authoritative UK guidance. The removal instruction "use a tick removal tool and twist gently" is confirmed by RSPCA, Blue Cross, and O'Tom manufacturer guidance. Safe to proceed to Developer.

## Alert 2 — Oak Processionary Moth

**Copy reviewed:**
"Oak processionary moth caterpillars are active on oak trees this time of year - look out for white silken nests on trunks and branches. Keep [dog name] away from any nests you spot, and do not let them sniff or paw at the bark underneath. If [dog name] has been near one, give your vet a call for advice."

**Seasonal note reviewed:** "Active May to July, mainly SE England and London."

---

### Claim-by-Claim Verification

**Claim 2.1**
"Oak processionary moth caterpillars are active on oak trees this time of year..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Caterpillar stage is May-July. The trigger is set to month 5-7. Accurate framing.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (OPM): "Seasonal window: May-July (caterpillar stage)."

---

**Claim 2.2**
"...look out for white silken nests on trunks and branches."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research confirms "The silken nests (white, pear-shaped) are visible on oak tree trunks and branches." The description is accurate.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (OPM).

---

**Claim 2.3**
"Keep [dog name] away from any nests you spot, and do not let them sniff or paw at the bark underneath."
Verdict: **INCOMPLETE**
Reasoning: The instruction limits the hazard zone to the nests and the bark directly underneath them. This understates the scope of the risk in two ways. First, the research explicitly states: "Even contact with shed hairs on the ground is hazardous." Second, Forest Research guidance confirms: "Caterpillars and nests can be on the ground, and animals can suffer considerable distress from hairs in their mouths and noses." Third: "The risk is possible at any time of year due to residual hairs from old nests." Ground-level hairs - not just the nests and the bark directly underneath - are a confirmed hazard. A dog walking through fallen hairs on the ground beneath or near an oak tree is at risk. The copy as written implies that keeping a dog away from the nest itself and the bark below it is sufficient protection, which is not accurate.
Fix required: The instruction should extend the hazard zone beyond the nest and the bark underneath. For example: "Keep [dog name] away from any nests you spot and from the ground beneath affected trees."
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (OPM): "Even contact with shed hairs on the ground is hazardous. Dead nests retain hazardous hairs for months." Forest Research OPM Manual 2: Public and Animal Health Advice.

---

**Claim 2.4**
"If [dog name] has been near one, give your vet a call for advice."
Verdict: **INCOMPLETE**
Reasoning: "Give your vet a call for advice" is the correct standard response per Blue Cross guidance ("call your vet for advice"). However, the research document rates this hazard as HIGH severity with the note "can cause anaphylaxis." The research's own action guidance states: "If contact suspected: flush mouth and eyes with water, seek emergency vet attention." Forest Research guidance adds: "Do NOT delay seeking veterinary care. The irritating hairs can cause severe swelling in the mouth, nose, and throat, potentially leading to breathing difficulties." The copy omits the immediate first-aid step (flush mouth and eyes with water) that should be taken before calling the vet. It also uses "give your vet a call for advice" which implies a non-urgent enquiry, whereas the research source and Forest Research specify emergency vet attention is the correct response. For a hazard rated HIGH with anaphylaxis noted as a possible outcome, this framing is insufficient.
Fix required: Two adjustments needed. (1) Add the flush-with-water first-aid step. (2) Elevate the urgency of the vet contact instruction. For example: "If [dog name] has been near one, rinse their mouth and eyes with clean water and contact your vet straight away."
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (OPM): "If contact suspected: flush mouth and eyes with water, seek emergency vet attention." Forest Research OPM Manual 2.

---

**Seasonal note 2.5**
"Active May to July, mainly SE England and London."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Both the seasonal window and geographic scope are confirmed. Research: "Seasonal window: May-July." "Primarily SE England and Greater London." The geographic qualifier is important and correctly included.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (OPM) and Gap Analysis Section 2.

---

### Overall Verdict: FLAG

Two issues identified, both in the same sentence. The hazard zone is understated (nests and bark only; ground hairs omitted). The emergency action is undersold ("give your vet a call" vs. the correct instruction to rinse immediately and contact the vet without delay). Both issues are fixable. Must not proceed to Developer without revision.

## Alert 3 — Lungworm

**Copy reviewed:**
"[Dog name] can pick up lungworm from eating slugs or snails - worth actively discouraging that habit, especially on damp evening walks. If [dog name] is not already on monthly lungworm prevention, it is worth a quick chat with your vet."

**Seasonal note reviewed:** "Peak risk August to October, but present year-round."

---

### Claim-by-Claim Verification

**Claim 3.1**
"[Dog name] can pick up lungworm from eating slugs or snails..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Transmission via ingestion of infected slugs or snails is confirmed. The research notes frogs as an additional (less common) transmission route, but slugs and snails are the primary vectors and appropriate for in-app brevity.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Lungworm): "transmitted via accidental ingestion of infected slugs, snails, or frog."

---

**Claim 3.2**
"...especially on damp evening walks."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Slugs and snails are most active in damp conditions and at dusk/evening. The terrain context (damp evening walks) is accurate and adds useful specificity to the action.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Lungworm): "Terrain: Woodland, gardens, any damp ground with slug/snail activity."

---

**Claim 3.3**
"If [dog name] is not already on monthly lungworm prevention, it is worth a quick chat with your vet."
Verdict: **INCOMPLETE**
Reasoning: This claim contains a critical safety gap. The phrase "monthly lungworm prevention" will be interpreted by many dog owners as their existing monthly worming product. Standard worming products containing fenbendazole (Panacur, Bob Martin, Drontal - widely used in the UK) do NOT prevent lungworm. The research states explicitly: "Standard worming (fenbendazole) does NOT prevent lungworm. Prevention requires milbemycin oxime (Milbemax) or moxidectin (Advocate)." A dog owner who worms their dog monthly with a standard product will read this sentence, believe their dog is already covered, and take no action - when in fact their dog may have no protection against lungworm at all. This is the most significant safety gap in the copy. The instruction is not wrong in itself - asking owners to check with their vet is correct - but it fails to flag that standard worming is not sufficient, which is the most likely misunderstanding.
Fix required: Add a qualifier that makes clear standard worming products are not sufficient. For example: "If [dog name] is not already on a specific lungworm preventative - standard worming products do not cover it - it is worth a quick chat with your vet."
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Lungworm): "Standard worming (fenbendazole) does NOT prevent lungworm."

---

**Seasonal note 3.4**
"Peak risk August to October, but present year-round."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research confirms: "Year-round; peak summer-autumn when slugs are most abundant." The note accurately conveys both the peak window and the year-round nature of the risk.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Lungworm) and Summary Table.

---

### Overall Verdict: FLAG

One issue identified but it is a safety-critical gap. An owner who already uses standard monthly worming will incorrectly conclude their dog is protected when they are not. The fix is a single parenthetical clause. Must not proceed to Developer without this revision.

## Alert 4 — Fungi and Mushrooms

**Copy reviewed:**
"Autumn mushrooms are appearing in woodland and grassland across the UK. Keep [dog name] away from any mushrooms on the ground - some UK species are highly toxic and symptoms can take several hours to show. If you think [dog name] has eaten one, call your vet straight away and do not wait for signs."

**Seasonal note reviewed:** "Active August to November."

---

### Claim-by-Claim Verification

**Claim 4.1**
"Autumn mushrooms are appearing in woodland and grassland across the UK."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: The seasonal window (Aug-Nov, peak Sep-Oct) and the terrains (woodland, grassland) are both confirmed. "Across the UK" is accurate - the hazard is nationwide, not regionally limited.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Fungi): "Seasonal window: August-November (peak September-October). Terrain: Broadleaved woodland, coniferous woodland, churchyards, anywhere with decaying organic matter."

---

**Claim 4.2**
"...some UK species are highly toxic..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Accurate. The death cap (Amanita phalloides) is described in the research as "most deadly fungus in the world." Funeral bell contains the same toxins. "Highly toxic" is appropriate severity language per the tone guide vocabulary (this alert rates as CRITICAL severity).
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Fungi): "Death cap... Most deadly fungus in the world."

---

**Claim 4.3**
"...symptoms can take several hours to show."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: The research states symptoms are "delayed 6-24 hours; initial vomiting and diarrhoea followed by apparent recovery, then liver and kidney failure." "Several hours" is an accurate lower-bound representation of the delay. The key safety instruction that follows - "do not wait for signs" - correctly addresses the dangerous implication of any delay. Together the claim and the instruction are accurate and appropriately urgent.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Fungi): "Symptoms delayed 6-24 hours."

---

**Claim 4.4**
"...call your vet straight away and do not wait for signs."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: The instruction "call your vet straight away" and "do not wait for signs" directly reflects the research guidance: "Seek emergency vet treatment immediately if mushroom ingestion suspected. Do not wait for symptoms. Early decontamination is critical." The phrase "do not wait for signs" is a critical safety element given the delayed onset of the most serious symptoms. Both elements are present and correct.
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 (Fungi): "Seek emergency vet treatment immediately if mushroom ingestion suspected. Do not wait for symptoms."

---

**Seasonal note 4.5**
"Active August to November."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Consistent with research: "August-November."
Source: Hazard research, Section 1 and Summary Table.

---

### Overall Verdict: PASS

All claims correct. The critical safety instruction ("do not wait for signs") is present and accurately placed. Safe to proceed to Developer.

## Alert 5 — Antifreeze

**Copy reviewed:**
"Antifreeze is used on driveways and car parks over winter and tastes sweet to dogs. Keep [dog name] away from puddles near parked cars and along roadsides on your walk. If you think [dog name] has licked any, get to a vet immediately - do not wait to see how they seem."

**Seasonal note reviewed:** "Higher risk November to March."

---

### Claim-by-Claim Verification

**Claim 5.1**
"Antifreeze is used on driveways and car parks over winter..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Accurate. Driveways and car parks are confirmed as primary exposure locations. The research also notes garages, roadsides, and garden water features as sources. For in-app brevity, driveways and car parks are the two highest-risk locations and are appropriate to lead with.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Antifreeze): "Found in: car antifreeze, screen wash, brake fluid, radiator coolant... Highest risk: spills in car parks, driveways after car maintenance, puddles under parked cars."

---

**Claim 5.2**
"...and tastes sweet to dogs."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Confirmed. Research: "Ethylene glycol tastes sweet, which actively attracts dogs." This is a critical contextual fact that explains why dogs are drawn to it and why owners need to be actively vigilant rather than relying on a dog's instinct to avoid it.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Antifreeze).

---

**Claim 5.3**
"Keep [dog name] away from puddles near parked cars and along roadsides on your walk."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Both locations are confirmed hazard zones. The research: "puddles under parked cars" is listed explicitly as the highest risk location. Roadsides are confirmed as urban/suburban terrain for this hazard.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Antifreeze).

---

**Claim 5.4**
"If you think [dog name] has licked any, get to a vet immediately..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Confirmed. Research: "Emergency vet immediately. This is one of the most time-critical poisoning scenarios." The instruction to go to a vet (not call a vet) is appropriately urgent for a hazard where treatment must begin within 12 hours.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Antifreeze): "Action: Emergency vet immediately."

---

**Claim 5.5**
"...do not wait to see how they seem."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: This instruction directly addresses the specific deception of antifreeze poisoning: a brief apparent recovery period follows initial symptoms before acute kidney failure develops within 24-72 hours. The research states: "Symptoms appear within 30-60 minutes: stumbling/ataxia, lethargy, vomiting. Brief apparent recovery period followed by acute kidney failure." The "do not wait" instruction is therefore not generic caution - it is a direct counter to the specific mechanism that causes owners to delay. Accurate and essential.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Antifreeze).

---

**Seasonal note 5.6**
"Higher risk November to March."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research: "November-March (peak December-February around car maintenance season)." The note is accurate and the qualifier "higher risk" correctly implies the hazard is present at other times too (screen wash, brake fluid are year-round products).
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Antifreeze).

---

### Overall Verdict: PASS

All claims correct. Both critical instructions ("get to a vet immediately" and "do not wait to see how they seem") are present, accurate, and correctly calibrated to the specific mechanism of antifreeze poisoning. Safe to proceed to Developer.

## Alert 6 — Cattle

**Copy reviewed:**
"Cattle can be unpredictable around dogs, especially during calving season in spring. Keep [dog name] on a short lead when walking through fields with cattle nearby. If cattle start to approach, let [dog name] off the lead and walk calmly to the nearest gate or field edge."

**Seasonal note reviewed:** "Year-round presence - highest risk March to June during calving."

---

### Claim-by-Claim Verification

**Claim 6.1**
"Cattle can be unpredictable around dogs..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research: "Cattle perceive dogs as predators." The characterisation of unpredictability is accurate and appropriate. Multiple official sources (HSE, Ramblers, Kennel Club) confirm cattle as a serious and unpredictable hazard around dogs.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Cattle).

---

**Claim 6.2**
"...especially during calving season in spring."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research: "highest risk March-June (calving season, spring protectiveness)." The seasonal qualifier is accurate. Cows with calves are confirmed as particularly defensive.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Cattle): "cows with calves are particularly defensive."

---

**Claim 6.3**
"Keep [dog name] on a short lead when walking through fields with cattle nearby."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research: "Keep dog on lead when crossing cattle fields." Kennel Club: "Let your dog off the lead if you are threatened by cattle" - the counterpart instruction confirming leads are standard prior to threat. HSE: "It is good practice to keep dogs on a lead when walking in rural areas where livestock are kept." All sources confirm lead control as the standard preventive measure.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Cattle). HSE AIS17 guidance. Kennel Club cattle safety guidance.

---

**Claim 6.4**
"If cattle start to approach, let [dog name] off the lead..."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: The instruction to release the dog if cattle approach is confirmed by all authoritative sources. Royal Kennel Club: "Let your dog off the lead if you are threatened by cattle." HSE: "let go of the lead if chased by cattle." Ramblers Association: "If you are threatened by cattle - let it go as the cattle will chase the dog." NFU/Kennel Club partnership signage: "let go if chased by cattle." The copy's "if cattle start to approach" is a reasonable trigger point consistent with "if you feel threatened" used by several sources.
Source: Royal Kennel Club cattle safety guidance; HSE AIS17; Ramblers Association guidance; Hazard research, Section 2 (Cattle).

---

**Claim 6.5**
"...walk calmly to the nearest gate or field edge."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research: "Move calmly to the field edge." The addition of "nearest gate" in the copy is accurate and practically useful - gates are the standard exit point from fields on public footpaths. "Walk calmly" correctly implies a measured exit.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Cattle): "Move calmly to the field edge."

---

**Claim 6.6 - MISSING INSTRUCTION**
"Do not run."
Verdict: **INCOMPLETE**
Reasoning: The copy says "walk calmly" which partially implies not running, but no authoritative source on this hazard leaves the "do not run" instruction implicit. Every official source treats this as an explicit standalone instruction because running is the most instinctive response under threat and the most dangerous one: cattle can run faster than humans and running triggers a chase response. NFU/Kennel Club signage: "Don't panic or run. Most cattle will stop before they reach you." Countryfile and HSE guidance both include "do not run" as a distinct instruction. The omission of an explicit prohibition on running is a material gap in a critical safety scenario. "Walk calmly" does not carry the same weight as the explicit instruction not to run, because a frightened person reading "walk calmly" in a charging cattle scenario may default to running once they feel the distance to the gate is sufficient. The explicit instruction must be present.
Fix required: Add "do not run" as an explicit instruction. For example: "...let [dog name] off the lead, do not run, and walk calmly to the nearest gate or field edge."
Source: NFU/Kennel Club cattle safety signage; HSE AIS17; Countryfile; Ramblers Association guidance.

---

**Seasonal note 6.7**
"Year-round presence - highest risk March to June during calving."
Verdict: **CORRECT**
Reasoning: Research: "Year-round; highest risk March-June (calving season)." The note is accurate.
Source: Hazard research, Section 2 (Cattle).

---

### Overall Verdict: FLAG

One issue identified. The explicit "do not run" instruction is absent. All authoritative sources include this as a standalone instruction, not as something implied by "walk calmly." Running during a cattle charge is the most dangerous response and the most instinctive one - the prohibition must be explicit. Fix is a four-word addition. Must not proceed to Developer without this revision.
